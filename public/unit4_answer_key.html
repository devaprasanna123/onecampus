Q1
A reaction shows reactants at 100 kJ/mol, products at 40 kJ/mol, and a peak at 160 kJ/mol. Determine the nature of the reaction, energy change, and activation energy.
ExothermicActivation EnergyEnergy Profile
Nature of Reaction

This is an exothermic reaction because the energy of the products (40 kJ/mol) is lower than that of the reactants (100 kJ/mol). Energy is released to the surroundings as the reaction proceeds.

Formulae & Calculations
ΔH (Energy Change) = Energy of Products − Energy of Reactants = 40 − 100 = −60 kJ/mol (negative → exothermic) Ea (Activation Energy) = Peak Energy − Energy of Reactants = 160 − 100 = +60 kJ/mol
Key Points
  • A negative ΔH confirms energy release — reaction is thermodynamically favorable.
  • Activation energy (60 kJ/mol) is the minimum energy molecules must possess to react.
  • The peak represents the transition state — a high-energy, unstable configuration.
  • Despite being exothermic, the reaction may still be slow if Ea is high relative to average molecular kinetic energy.
Summary
The reaction is exothermic (ΔH = −60 kJ/mol) with an activation barrier of 60 kJ/mol. A favorable energy change does not guarantee a fast reaction — the activation energy governs rate, while ΔH governs spontaneity.
Q2
Reactants at 200 kJ/mol, products at 80 kJ/mol, peak at 350 kJ/mol. Reaction proceeds very slowly. Analyze the discrepancy between favorable energy change and slow rate.
High Activation EnergyKinetics vs Thermodynamics
Analysis

Although the reaction is thermodynamically favorable (ΔH = −120 kJ/mol, exothermic), it proceeds very slowly. The dominant controlling factor is the extremely high activation energy.

Formulae & Calculations
ΔH = 80 − 200 = −120 kJ/mol (exothermic, thermodynamically favorable) Ea = 350 − 200 = +150 kJ/mol (very high barrier) Rate ∝ e^(−Ea/RT) [Arrhenius equation — rate decreases exponentially with high Ea]
Key Points
  • Thermodynamic feasibility (ΔH < 0) and kinetic rate are independent concepts.
  • A large Ea means very few molecules have sufficient energy to reach the transition state at room temperature.
  • The Arrhenius equation shows rate decreases exponentially as Ea increases.
  • This explains why many combustion reactions do not occur spontaneously at room temperature without ignition.
Summary
The reaction is thermodynamically spontaneous but kinetically hindered. The extremely high activation energy (150 kJ/mol) means virtually no molecules at room temperature possess sufficient energy to cross the barrier, making the rate negligibly slow despite a favorable energy change.
Q3
Reactants at 150 kJ/mol, products at 90 kJ/mol, peak at 300 kJ/mol. Slow and energy-intensive. Engineers want to improve efficiency without altering products. Recommend a strategy.
CatalystEnergy Profile Modification
Strategy

The recommended strategy is to introduce a catalyst. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, thereby increasing the reaction rate without altering the reactants, products, or the overall ΔH.

Formulae & Effect
Original: Ea = 300 − 150 = 150 kJ/mol Catalyzed: Ea (new) < 150 kJ/mol (e.g., reduced to 80 kJ/mol) ΔH remains = 90 − 150 = −60 kJ/mol (catalyst does NOT change ΔH) Rate increase: k = A·e^(−Ea/RT) → lower Ea → exponentially higher k
Key Points
  • A catalyst lowers Ea by providing a new mechanism (different transition state or intermediate).
  • The energy levels of reactants and products remain unchanged — ΔH is unaffected.
  • The catalyst is not consumed and can be recovered at the end.
  • Other strategies (increasing temperature) also work but increase energy consumption and may affect product stability.
  • Industrial example: iron catalyst in Haber process for ammonia synthesis.
Summary
Introducing a catalyst is the ideal strategy. It lowers the activation energy, increases reaction rate, reduces energy consumption, and leaves the product yield at equilibrium unchanged. The energy profile shifts the peak downward while reactant and product energy levels stay fixed.
Q4
Reaction A: low peak, small energy difference. Reaction B: high peak, large energy difference. Both under identical conditions. Which is faster? Which is more stable?
Activation EnergyThermodynamic StabilityKinetics
Definitions

Activation Energy (Ea): Minimum energy required for reactants to transform into products (height of the energy peak above reactant level).

Thermodynamic Stability: Governed by ΔH — larger negative ΔH means products are more stable relative to reactants.

Analysis
Reaction A: Low Ea → FASTER rate (more molecules can cross barrier) Small ΔH → Less thermodynamically stable products Reaction B: High Ea → SLOWER rate Large ΔH → More thermodynamically stable products (lower energy)
Key Points
  • Speed is governed by Ea (kinetics) — Reaction A is faster.
  • Stability is governed by ΔH (thermodynamics) — Reaction B produces more stable products.
  • This illustrates kinetic vs thermodynamic control of reactions.
  • At low temperatures, kinetically controlled products dominate; at high temperatures, thermodynamic products dominate.
Summary
Reaction A (low Ea) is faster due to a lower activation barrier. Reaction B (large ΔH) produces more thermodynamically stable products. These two properties are independent — a reaction can be fast but produce less stable products, or slow but produce very stable products.
Q5
Process X: low Ea, low energy output. Process Y: high Ea, high energy output. For continuous large-scale production, which is more efficient?
Industrial EfficiencyTrade-off Analysis
Trade-off Analysis

Process X (Low Ea, Low Output): Faster reaction, lower energy input to initiate, but lower energy yield per cycle. Suitable for rapid, continuous production where speed matters more than output per batch.

Process Y (High Ea, High Output): Slower reaction, higher energy input required, but greater energy yield per cycle. May require a catalyst to be economically viable.

Key Points
  • For continuous large-scale production, Process X is preferred due to lower activation energy, enabling sustained throughput at lower operational cost.
  • Process Y can be made viable by using a catalyst to lower Ea while retaining high energy output.
  • Overall efficiency = (energy output − energy input) / energy input × 100%.
  • Industrial considerations also include selectivity, by-product formation, and equipment lifespan.
Summary
For continuous large-scale production, Process X (low Ea) is more practically efficient because lower activation energy translates to lower heating costs, faster reaction, and greater throughput. However, if high energy output is a critical requirement, Process Y combined with appropriate catalysis becomes preferable.
Q6
An energy profile shows reactants higher in energy than products with a single peak. Infer the reaction type and justify using energy profile characteristics.
ExothermicEnergy Profile Reading
Inference

The reaction is exothermic. When reactants have higher energy than products, the energy difference is released as heat (or other forms of energy) to the surroundings during the reaction.

Energy Profile Features
Reactants ──▲── Transition State \ └──── Products (lower energy) ΔH = E(products) − E(reactants) < 0 → Exothermic Single peak = single-step reaction with one transition state
Key Points
  • A downward slope from reactants to products confirms energy release → exothermic.
  • The single peak represents the one transition state in this elementary reaction.
  • The peak height above reactants = Ea (forward reaction).
  • The peak height above products = Ea (reverse reaction) — always larger in exothermic reactions.
  • Examples: combustion, neutralization, oxidation of metals.
Summary
The profile describes an exothermic reaction with a single transition state. Reactants are at higher potential energy; the system releases energy as it proceeds to lower-energy products. The single peak indicates an elementary (one-step) mechanism.
Q7
A reaction shows a very high peak followed by a steep drop in energy. It releases large energy quickly. Assess suitability considering safety and feasibility.
Safety AnalysisExplosive ReactionsFeasibility
Characterization

This profile describes a highly exothermic reaction with high activation energy — typical of explosive or combustion reactions. Once the energy barrier is crossed (ignition), an enormous amount of energy is rapidly released.

Safety vs. Feasibility
High Ea → Reaction is kinetically stable (does NOT self-initiate) Large ΔH → Massive energy release once initiated (safety hazard) Energy released rapidly → temperature spike → potential runaway reaction
Key Points
  • The high Ea makes the reaction safe to store and handle under normal conditions (kinetically stable).
  • Once the activation barrier is overcome (heat, spark, shock), the large ΔH causes rapid, uncontrollable energy release.
  • Safety concerns: explosion risk, thermal runaway, rapid gas evolution.
  • Feasibility: thermodynamically very favorable, but must be controlled (e.g., controlled combustion in engines).
  • Industrial use requires pressure vessels, controlled ignition, and heat management systems.
Summary
The reaction is thermodynamically feasible (large negative ΔH) and kinetically stable (high Ea), but poses significant safety risks once initiated. Controlled use in engines or industrial reactors is possible with proper ignition control and energy management. Uncontrolled initiation is extremely hazardous.
Q8
A thermodynamically favorable reaction proceeds slowly at room temperature due to a high energy barrier (transition state). Additional heating increases rate. Analyze the energy barrier's role.
Transition StateTemperature EffectArrhenius
Role of the Energy Barrier

The energy barrier (activation energy) acts as a kinetic gate that controls how quickly reactants are converted to products, irrespective of thermodynamic favorability. The transition state at the peak represents an unstable, high-energy configuration that molecules must pass through.

Formulae
Arrhenius Equation: k = A · e^(−Ea/RT) k = rate constant A = frequency/pre-exponential factor Ea = activation energy (J/mol) R = 8.314 J/mol·K T = absolute temperature (K) At higher T: e^(−Ea/RT) increases → k increases → faster reaction Maxwell-Boltzmann: At higher T, more molecules have E ≥ Ea
Key Points
  • The transition state is a fleeting, high-energy species at the peak — not an intermediate, cannot be isolated.
  • At room temperature, few molecules have kinetic energy ≥ Ea → slow rate.
  • Heating increases average kinetic energy and broadens the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution → more molecules exceed Ea.
  • As a rule of thumb, a 10°C rise doubles the reaction rate for many reactions.
  • The transition state's energy above reactants defines Ea; its energy above products defines the reverse Ea.
Summary
The energy barrier (Ea) acts as a kinetic control mechanism. The high-energy transition state at the peak must be reached for products to form. Additional heating shifts the molecular energy distribution, dramatically increasing the fraction of molecules capable of crossing the barrier, thus accelerating the reaction.
Q9
A reversible reaction has different forward and reverse activation energies. Determine how this governs directionality and equilibrium behavior.
Reversible ReactionEquilibriumForward & Reverse Ea
Concept

In a reversible reaction, the forward and reverse reactions have the same transition state but different activation energies, because the reactants and products are at different energy levels.

Formulae & Relationships
Ea(forward) = E(transition state) − E(reactants) Ea(reverse) = E(transition state) − E(products) Relationship: ΔH = Ea(forward) − Ea(reverse) If Ea(fwd) < Ea(rev): Forward reaction faster → exothermic (ΔH < 0) If Ea(fwd) > Ea(rev): Reverse reaction faster → endothermic (ΔH > 0) At equilibrium: Rate(forward) = Rate(reverse) k(fwd)/k(rev) = Keq = e^(−ΔH/RT) [simplified]
Key Points
  • The smaller Ea determines which direction is kinetically favored — that reaction proceeds faster.
  • Equilibrium is reached when forward and reverse rates are equal, not when Ea values are equal.
  • A large ΔH (large difference between Ea values) favors one direction strongly → equilibrium lies far toward products or reactants.
  • Catalyst lowers both Ea(fwd) and Ea(rev) equally → equilibrium position unchanged, both rates increase.
Summary
The difference between forward and reverse activation energies equals ΔH of the reaction. The reaction with smaller Ea proceeds faster, determining directionality under kinetic conditions. At equilibrium, both forward and reverse rates are equal, with position determined by the relative energy levels (thermodynamics), not by the Ea values alone.
Q10
Reaction with strong covalent bond breaking is slower than one with weaker intermolecular interactions. Molecular orientation also affects success. Analyze bond strength and orientation's influence on Ea.
Bond StrengthMolecular OrientationCollision Theory
Collision Theory Framework

According to collision theory, a reaction occurs when molecules collide with (1) sufficient energy ≥ Ea and (2) correct geometric orientation. Both bond strength and molecular orientation directly determine how easily these conditions are met.

Formulae & Concepts
Rate = Z · p · e^(−Ea/RT) Z = collision frequency p = steric factor (probability of correct orientation, 0 < p ≤ 1) e^(−Ea/RT) = fraction of collisions with energy ≥ Ea Bond Energy Effect: Strong covalent bonds (e.g., C–C, ~347 kJ/mol) → high Ea Weak van der Waals (~5–40 kJ/mol) → low Ea Orientation Effect: Wrong orientation → p ≈ 0 (collision is ineffective) Correct orientation → p close to 1
Key Points
  • Stronger bonds require more energy to break → higher Ea → slower rate at the same temperature.
  • Weak intermolecular forces require less energy to overcome → lower Ea → faster rate.
  • The steric factor (p) accounts for orientation: complex molecules with specific binding sites have lower p → slower rate despite sufficient energy.
  • Both effects act simultaneously — a strongly bonded molecule that also requires precise orientation has an exceptionally slow rate.
Summary
Bond strength raises Ea by increasing the energy needed for bond rearrangement, while poor molecular orientation reduces the steric factor (p). The reaction involving strong covalent bonds is slower because both its Ea is higher and the probability of a productive collision is lower. Together, these factors explain observed rate differences.
Q11
Increasing temperature accelerates a reaction significantly. Interpret how temperature alters molecular energy distribution and its effect on activation energy crossing probability.
Maxwell-BoltzmannTemperatureArrhenius
Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution

At any temperature, molecules have a distribution of kinetic energies described by the Maxwell-Boltzmann curve. Only the fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea can react. Raising temperature shifts this distribution to higher energies and broadens it.

Formulae
Arrhenius: k = A · e^(−Ea/RT) At T₁: k₁ = A · e^(−Ea/RT₁) At T₂: k₂ = A · e^(−Ea/RT₂) (T₂ > T₁) ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R) · (1/T₁ − 1/T₂) Maxwell-Boltzmann: f(E) = 2π(1/πkT)^(3/2) · √E · e^(−E/kT) Fraction with E ≥ Ea ∝ e^(−Ea/RT) → increases rapidly with T
Key Points
  • Higher T → peak of distribution shifts right → more molecules with high kinetic energy.
  • The area under the Maxwell-Boltzmann curve to the right of Ea represents reactive molecules — this area grows significantly with temperature.
  • Temperature does NOT change the value of Ea; it changes the fraction of molecules that can overcome it.
  • A 10 K increase can double the rate because of the exponential dependence in the Arrhenius equation.
  • Higher T also increases collision frequency Z, but the exponential term dominates.
Summary
Temperature increases the average kinetic energy of molecules and shifts the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution to higher energies. This exponentially increases the fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea, dramatically increasing the rate constant and hence the reaction rate. The activation energy itself is not altered by temperature.
Q12
A short-lived, unstable configuration at the highest energy point in the reaction pathway converts into products. Infer its significance and relationship with activation energy.
Transition StateActivated Complex
Definition

The unstable configuration at the highest energy point is called the transition state or activated complex. It is the configuration of atoms at the energy maximum along the reaction coordinate.

Key Relationships
Ea = E(transition state) − E(reactants) Properties of the Transition State: • Highest energy point on the potential energy surface • Cannot be isolated — exists for ~10⁻¹³ seconds • Bonds are partially broken and partially formed • Species is neither reactant nor product • Defined by Transition State Theory (TST)
Key Points
  • The transition state is NOT a reaction intermediate — it has no finite lifetime and cannot be isolated or detected directly.
  • The energy difference between reactants and the transition state defines Ea (forward).
  • A lower-energy transition state (achieved via catalyst or better orbital overlap) means lower Ea and faster reaction.
  • Distinct from reaction intermediates, which are local energy minima between steps in a multi-step mechanism.
Summary
The transition state (activated complex) is the highest-energy, most unstable configuration in the reaction pathway. Its energy above the reactants defines the activation energy. It exists for an immeasurably short time, with bonds in the process of breaking and forming. Lowering the energy of the transition state is the key mechanism by which catalysts accelerate reactions.
Q13
An energy profile diagram shows reactants at a higher energy level and products at a lower energy level with a noticeable peak. Determine the significance of the peak and its role in reaction kinetics.
Energy ProfileReaction KineticsActivation Energy
Significance of the Peak

The peak in the energy profile represents the transition state (activated complex) — the highest energy configuration along the reaction coordinate. Its position and height are central to understanding both the rate and direction of the reaction.

Key Parameters from the Profile
Ea (forward) = Peak height − Reactant energy [determines rate of forward rxn] Ea (reverse) = Peak height − Product energy [determines rate of reverse rxn] ΔH = Product energy − Reactant energy [negative here → exothermic] Kinetic role: Only molecules with KE ≥ Ea(fwd) can react Thermodynamic role: ΔH determines heat exchange with surroundings
Key Points
  • The peak height above reactants = Ea(fwd) — determines how fast the forward reaction proceeds.
  • A higher peak = slower reaction rate at a given temperature.
  • The peak represents the transition state — a maximum in potential energy, not a stable species.
  • Catalysts lower the peak, increasing reaction rate without changing the energy levels of reactants or products.
  • The peak is used to read off activation energies graphically.
Summary
The peak in an energy profile diagram signifies the transition state. Its height above reactants gives the forward activation energy (rate-determining factor), while its height above products gives the reverse activation energy. The overall energy difference (ΔH) between reactants and products determines reaction spontaneity and heat exchange, independent of the peak height.
Q14
Two reactions at the same temperature: one much faster due to lower energy requirement for bond rearrangement. Analyze the reason for speed difference based on energy considerations.
Activation EnergyRate ConstantArrhenius
Analysis

At the same temperature, both reactions have molecules with the same average kinetic energy. The difference in rate arises entirely from the difference in activation energy (Ea) — specifically, the energy needed for bond rearrangement at the transition state.

Quantitative Comparison
At temperature T: k₁ = A·e^(−Ea₁/RT) [slower reaction, higher Ea₁] k₂ = A·e^(−Ea₂/RT) [faster reaction, lower Ea₂] k₂/k₁ = e^((Ea₁ − Ea₂)/RT) Example: If Ea₁ − Ea₂ = 20 kJ/mol at 300 K: k₂/k₁ = e^(20000/(8.314×300)) ≈ e^8 ≈ 2981× (nearly 3000× faster!)
Key Points
  • A small difference in Ea leads to a very large difference in rate due to the exponential relationship.
  • Lower Ea means fewer bonds need to be broken or rearranged, or bonds are weaker/more polarized.
  • At the same T, all energy comes from the molecular kinetic energy distribution — more molecules exceed lower Ea.
  • This is why catalysts (which lower Ea) are so effective: even a modest reduction in Ea causes enormous rate enhancement.
Summary
The faster reaction requires less energy for bond rearrangement, meaning a lower Ea. Because rate depends exponentially on −Ea/RT (Arrhenius), even a modest reduction in activation energy leads to dramatically higher reaction rates at the same temperature. The difference arises purely from how much energy is needed to reach the transition state.
Q15
An iron-based catalyst is introduced into ammonia synthesis. Rate increases significantly without raising T or P. Catalyst remains unchanged. Energy profile shows a reduced peak. Analyze how the catalyst modifies the reaction pathway.
Heterogeneous CatalysisHaber ProcessAlternative Pathway
Mechanism of Catalytic Action

The iron catalyst accelerates ammonia synthesis by providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy. It does not change the thermodynamics (ΔH, equilibrium position) of the reaction, only the kinetics.

Steps & Energy Analysis
Uncatalyzed: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ Ea ≈ 335 kJ/mol (very slow at room T) Catalyzed (Fe surface): Step 1: N₂ adsorbs on Fe surface → N₂(ads) [chemisorption] Step 2: N≡N bond weakened/broken → N(ads) [lower Ea step] Step 3: H₂ adsorbs → H(ads) Step 4: N(ads) + 3H(ads) → NH₃ [surface reaction] Step 5: NH₃ desorbs from surface Net Ea (catalyzed) ≈ 160 kJ/mol (much lower) ΔH remains: −92 kJ/mol (unchanged) Catalyst regenerated at Step 5 (desorption)
Key Points
  • The catalyst provides an alternative pathway — a series of lower-energy steps instead of one high-energy step.
  • The iron surface weakens the strong N≡N triple bond (945 kJ/mol), making nitrogen activation possible at industrial temperatures.
  • Promoters (K₂O, Al₂O₃) are added to enhance iron's catalytic activity (structural/electronic promoters).
  • The catalyst is recovered unchanged — it participates in the mechanism but is regenerated.
  • The equilibrium yield of NH₃ is unaffected — only the rate of reaching equilibrium changes.
Summary
The iron catalyst provides an alternative multi-step pathway for ammonia synthesis with significantly lower activation energy (~160 vs ~335 kJ/mol). By adsorbing N₂ and weakening the N≡N bond on its surface, it enables the reaction at practical industrial temperatures. The catalyst is unchanged after each cycle, and the equilibrium position (ΔH) remains the same — only the rate is enhanced.
Q16
An enzyme catalyzes only one type of substrate while ignoring similar molecules. Reaction is rapid in small quantities, no loss of activity after repeated cycles, and equilibrium position is unchanged. Interpret enzyme behavior.
Enzyme CatalysisSpecificityBiological Catalyst
Key Properties

Specificity: Enzymes are highly specific because of their unique three-dimensional active site that fits only one substrate (lock-and-key or induced-fit model).

Efficiency: Enzymes work in minute quantities because they are true catalysts — not consumed, regenerated after each cycle.

Equilibrium: Like all catalysts, enzymes do not shift equilibrium position — they only accelerate approach to equilibrium.

Mechanisms & Concepts
Lock-and-Key Model: Enzyme active site = specific shape Only matching substrate can bind Induced-Fit Model: Active site changes shape upon substrate binding More accurate for most enzymes Enzyme + Substrate → [Enzyme-Substrate Complex] → Products + Enzyme Turnover Number (TON): Number of substrate molecules converted per enzyme molecule per second (typically 100–1000 s⁻¹) Enzyme does NOT alter: ΔG°, Keq, ΔH of reaction Enzyme DOES alter: Ea (lowers it), reaction rate
Key Points
  • Specificity arises from the complementary shape and chemical properties of the active site and substrate.
  • Efficiency is measured by turnover number — enzymes can process thousands of molecules per second.
  • Enzymes work under mild conditions (body temperature, neutral pH) — industrial advantage.
  • No change in equilibrium position: enzymes speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally.
  • Industrial applications: proteases in detergents, amylases in food industry, lipases in biodiesel production.
Summary
Enzymes are highly specific biological catalysts that operate through precise active-site complementarity (lock-and-key / induced-fit). They are efficient (high TON), reusable, and do not alter the thermodynamic equilibrium of the reaction. Their industrial value lies in high selectivity, mild operating conditions, and the ability to minimize unwanted by-products.
Q17
Energy profiles plotted with and without catalyst. Catalyzed reaction shows lower peak; both start/end at same energy levels. Forward and reverse rates increase equally. Compare using energy profile data and determine effect on kinetics and equilibrium.
Catalyst EffectEnergy Profile ComparisonEquilibrium
Comparison

The energy profile comparison clearly shows that the catalyst lowers the activation energy for both the forward and reverse reactions by the same absolute amount, while leaving the energy levels of reactants and products unchanged.

Energy Profile Analysis
UNCATALYZED: Reactants ──▲── (high peak) ──── Products Ea(fwd) = H_peak − H_reactants Ea(rev) = H_peak − H_products CATALYZED (lower peak, same start/end): Reactants ──▲'── (lower peak) ──── Products Ea'(fwd) = Ea(fwd) − ΔEa [reduced] Ea'(rev) = Ea(rev) − ΔEa [reduced by same amount] ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants) = UNCHANGED in both cases Keq = k(fwd)/k(rev) = UNCHANGED (both rates increase proportionally)
Key Points
  • Catalyst lowers Ea for both directions by equal amounts → ΔH unchanged → Keq unchanged.
  • Both forward and reverse rates increase equally → equilibrium is reached faster but at the same position.
  • The catalyst creates an alternative pathway (different transition state at lower energy).
  • Catalyst does not appear in the overall stoichiometry of the balanced equation.
  • The selectivity of a catalyst can affect which products form in competing reactions, even without changing equilibrium.
Summary
A catalyst lowers the activation energy of both forward and reverse reactions by an identical amount, reducing the peak height on the energy profile while leaving reactant and product energy levels — and hence ΔH and Keq — unchanged. This accelerates both directions equally, meaning equilibrium is reached more quickly but the equilibrium composition is not altered.
Q18
A catalyst completes 500 reaction cycles per minute. Amount used is very small, no degradation. Researchers calculate a performance metric based on cycles per unit time. Determine the significance of turnover number.
Turnover NumberCatalytic PerformanceTON & TOF
Definitions

Turnover Number (TON): Total number of substrate (reactant) molecules converted per catalytic site over the entire lifetime of the catalyst.

Turnover Frequency (TOF): Number of catalytic cycles per unit time per catalytic site.

Formulae
TOF = Number of reactions per catalyst molecule per unit time = 500 cycles / min (given) = 500/60 ≈ 8.33 s⁻¹ TON = TOF × lifetime of catalyst (total cycles in full lifetime) Example: If catalyst lasts 10 hours: TON = 500 × 60 × 10 = 300,000 cycles per catalyst molecule Higher TON → More economical (less catalyst needed) Higher TOF → Faster reaction rate
Key Points
  • TON is a measure of catalyst longevity and economic viability — higher TON means catalyst is reused more before replacement.
  • TOF measures catalytic activity (rate) — higher TOF means faster conversion per unit time.
  • No degradation → catalyst regenerates perfectly each cycle → maximum TON possible.
  • Industrial catalysts are evaluated by TON, TOF, selectivity, and stability.
  • Enzymes typically have TOF of 100–10,000 s⁻¹; some engineered catalysts exceed 10⁶ s⁻¹.
Summary
The turnover number (TON) quantifies total catalyst productivity over its lifetime, while turnover frequency (TOF) measures its activity rate. A catalyst completing 500 cycles/min with no degradation demonstrates high efficiency and reusability. These metrics allow objective comparison of different catalysts and are critical for evaluating industrial feasibility and cost-effectiveness.
Q19
After introducing a catalyst, a reaction that required high temperature now occurs quickly at a lower temperature, reducing energy consumption. Equilibrium yield is unchanged. Analyze how the catalyst contributes to energy savings.
Energy SavingsCatalystEquilibrium Yield
Energy Savings Mechanism

By lowering Ea, the catalyst allows the same rate to be achieved at a significantly lower temperature. Since industrial heating is energy-intensive, this directly translates to reduced energy consumption (fuel savings) and lower operating costs.

Analysis
Without catalyst: Required T_high to achieve acceptable rate (high Ea) With catalyst: Same rate achieved at T_low (lower Ea via new pathway) Energy savings: Q = mcΔT [less heating required] No yield change: Keq = e^(−ΔG°/RT) — depends only on ΔG°, not on Ea → catalyst lowers Ea equally for fwd & rev → Keq unchanged Practical example (SO₃ synthesis): V₂O₅ catalyst allows ~450°C instead of ~700°C without catalyst
Key Points
  • Lower operating temperature = reduced fuel consumption = direct cost savings.
  • Lower temperature also means less thermal degradation of equipment and safer operations.
  • Equilibrium yield is unchanged because ΔG° is a thermodynamic property — independent of Ea and catalyst presence.
  • The catalyst makes the reaction kinetically accessible at temperatures where the equilibrium is more favorable.
  • This is the principle behind green chemistry — catalysis enables energy-efficient, lower-waste processes.
Summary
The catalyst reduces Ea, allowing adequate reaction rates at lower temperatures. This yields significant energy savings (less heating required) and reduces operational costs. The equilibrium yield remains unchanged because catalysts affect only kinetics, not the thermodynamic equilibrium position (Keq).
Q20
A catalyst accelerates only one specific reaction among several possible side reactions. Undesired products minimized. Catalyst is recovered. Infer the role of catalyst selectivity.
Catalyst SelectivitySide ReactionsProduct Control
Definition of Selectivity

Catalyst selectivity is the ability of a catalyst to preferentially accelerate one specific reaction pathway among several thermodynamically possible pathways, leading to preferential formation of a desired product.

Types of Selectivity
Chemoselectivity: Prefers one functional group over another (e.g., reduces C=O without affecting C=C) Regioselectivity: Prefers one position in a molecule (e.g., oxidizes primary vs secondary alcohol) Stereoselectivity: Produces one stereoisomer preferentially (e.g., chiral catalysts in asymmetric synthesis) Selectivity (%) = (Moles of desired product / Total moles reacted) × 100
Key Points
  • Selective catalysts reduce the activation energy for the desired pathway more than for competing pathways.
  • Minimizing side reactions improves atom economy and reduces waste — important for green chemistry.
  • The recovered catalyst can be reused, reducing material cost.
  • Enzymes are the ultimate selective catalysts — single substrate, single product, essentially zero side reactions.
  • Industrial importance: selective catalysts reduce purification costs and improve product purity.
Summary
Catalyst selectivity directs the reaction toward the desired product by preferentially lowering the activation energy of that specific pathway. This minimizes side-product formation, improves yield purity, reduces downstream separation costs, and contributes to sustainable chemical production. The catalyst's recoverability further enhances its industrial and economic value.
Q21
Enzymes regulate metabolic reactions efficiently under mild conditions. They function repeatedly and maintain reaction rates necessary for survival. Determine how biological catalysts maintain efficiency under mild conditions.
Enzyme CatalysisBiological SystemsMild Conditions
Mechanism of Enzyme Efficiency

Enzymes maintain high efficiency under mild biological conditions (37°C, pH ~7, atmospheric pressure) through multiple simultaneous mechanisms that collectively lower the activation energy far more than simple temperature increase could achieve.

Key Mechanisms
1. Proximity & Orientation Effect: Enzyme active site positions substrate in ideal orientation for reaction → Increases effective concentration and reaction probability 2. Strain/Distortion: Active site strains substrate bonds → easier to break (lower Ea) 3. Acid-Base Catalysis: Amino acid residues donate/accept protons to stabilize transition state 4. Covalent Catalysis: Enzyme forms temporary covalent bond with substrate → lower-energy pathway 5. Metal Ion Catalysis: Metal cofactors (Zn²⁺, Fe³⁺) facilitate electron transfer Result: Ea reduced by 30–100 kJ/mol compared to uncatalyzed reaction → Rate increases by 10⁶ to 10¹⁸ × at 37°C
Key Points
  • Enzymes can achieve at 37°C what would require hundreds of degrees in uncatalyzed reactions.
  • Their active site creates a unique microenvironment optimized for transition state stabilization.
  • Repeated functioning is possible because enzyme is regenerated in each catalytic cycle.
  • Cofactors (vitamins, metal ions) are often required for enzyme activity.

Q42
A reaction in an energy system requires selective conversion of one reactant among many similar compounds. The catalyst ensures only the desired product forms while minimizing side reactions. Determine the catalytic property responsible.
Catalyst SelectivityIndustrial ApplicationsSpecificity
Catalytic Property: Selectivity

The property responsible is catalyst selectivity — specifically chemoselectivity or substrate selectivity. The catalyst preferentially lowers the activation energy for the reaction of the desired substrate/pathway while leaving activation energies for competing reactions relatively unchanged.

Mechanism of Selectivity
Types of Selectivity in this Context: SUBSTRATE SELECTIVITY: Catalyst binds to only one specific substrate among similar molecules → Geometric fit (size/shape exclusion, like zeolite shape selectivity) → Chemical complementarity (electronic matching) PRODUCT SELECTIVITY: Among multiple possible products from one substrate, catalyst directs formation toward one specific product → One reaction pathway's Ea is lowered more than others REACTION SELECTIVITY: One reaction type (e.g., oxidation) favored over another (e.g., reduction) in a complex mixture Industrial Examples: • Zeolite catalysts in petroleum cracking: pore size controls which molecules can enter → shape selectivity • Selective catalytic reduction (SCR): NOₓ → N₂ (not N₂O) V₂O₅/TiO₂ catalyst selectively reduces NOₓ with NH₃ • Fuel cells: Pt catalyst selectively oxidizes H₂, not CO (at high T) Significance: Higher yield of desired product → better economics Fewer by-products → less separation cost, less waste Environmental compliance → fewer harmful emissions
Key Points
  • Selectivity is the most commercially valuable property of industrial catalysts — often more important than raw activity.
  • Shape-selective catalysts (zeolites) use pore geometry to physically exclude unwanted substrates or products.
  • Electronic selectivity arises from specific orbital overlap requirements at the active site.
  • High selectivity minimizes downstream processing costs — purification of product mixtures is expensive and waste-generating.
  • In energy systems (fuel cells, batteries), selectivity prevents degradation reactions that reduce efficiency and lifespan.
Summary
The catalytic property responsible is selectivity — the catalyst's ability to preferentially activate one substrate or one reaction pathway among many. This is achieved through a combination of geometric complementarity (shape selectivity), chemical specificity (electronic matching), and differential activation energy lowering. In industrial energy systems, high selectivity is critical for maximizing product yield, minimizing by-products, reducing separation costs, and ensuring long-term system efficiency and reliability.
