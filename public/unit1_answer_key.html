Analyze the origin of the observed potential difference in a Zinc-Copper electrochemical cell and infer how chemical reactions establish a driving force for electron movement while maintaining charge balance.
8 Marks▼
Answer

The setup described is the classic Daniell Cell, one of the earliest practical electrochemical cells. The potential difference originates from a difference in the tendency to lose electrons (oxidation potential) between zinc and copper.

At the Zinc electrode (Anode – Oxidation):
Zinc has a higher tendency to oxidize. Zinc metal atoms lose electrons and dissolve into the solution as Zn²⁺ ions.

Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ (E° = +0.76 V vs SHE)

At the Copper electrode (Cathode – Reduction):
The electrons released at the zinc electrode travel through the external circuit to the copper electrode, where Cu²⁺ ions from solution are reduced and deposited as copper metal.

Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) (E° = +0.34 V vs SHE)

Cell EMF:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V

Charge Balance via Salt Bridge:
As Zn²⁺ ions accumulate in the anode compartment, the solution would become positively charged, stopping the reaction. The salt bridge (containing KNO₃ or KCl) allows ion migration — anions (NO₃⁻) move into the anode compartment and cations (K⁺) move into the cathode compartment — thus maintaining electrical neutrality in both half-cells.

The driving force for electron movement is the difference in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) between the two half-reactions, which translates into an electrical potential difference (EMF).

ΔG = −nFE°cell where n = electrons transferred, F = Faraday's constant (96485 C/mol)
Key Points
  • Anode: oxidation (zinc dissolves); Cathode: reduction (copper deposits)
  • EMF arises due to difference in electrode potentials
  • Salt bridge completes the circuit by ionic conduction, maintains charge neutrality
  • No external power supply needed — energy from spontaneous redox reaction
  • ΔG = −nFE: negative ΔG confirms spontaneity
Summary
The EMF in a Zn-Cu cell originates from the difference in oxidation tendencies of the two metals. Electrons flow from Zn (anode) to Cu (cathode) through the external circuit, while ions migrate through the salt bridge to maintain charge balance, enabling sustained current flow.
2
Determine how variations in chemical conditions influence electrode potential and justify the thermodynamic basis for potential generation in electrochemical systems (Nernst Equation).
8 Marks▼
Answer

The electrode potential of a half-cell is not fixed — it depends on the concentration (activity) of the ions in solution, temperature, and pressure. This relationship is described by the Nernst Equation.

Nernst Equation for a half-cell:

E = E° − (RT/nF) × ln[Q]

At 298 K (25°C), using log₁₀:

E = E° − (0.0591/n) × log[Q]

Where: E° = standard electrode potential, R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T = temperature in Kelvin, n = number of electrons transferred, F = 96485 C/mol, Q = reaction quotient.

Thermodynamic Basis: The driving force for a redox reaction is the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG). Under non-standard conditions:

ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q

Since ΔG = −nFE and ΔG° = −nFE°, substituting:

−nFE = −nFE° + RT ln Q ⟹ E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

Effect of Concentration: Increasing the concentration of products decreases E; increasing reactant (ion) concentration increases E. For example, in the Zn²⁺/Zn half-cell, increasing [Zn²⁺] makes the electrode potential more positive (less negative), reducing the tendency to oxidize.

Key Points
  • Nernst Equation: E = E° − (0.0591/n) log Q at 298 K
  • Higher ion concentration → higher electrode potential (for cation-dependent reactions)
  • ΔG = −nFE is the thermodynamic basis
  • At equilibrium, E = 0 and Q = K (equilibrium constant)
  • Both cells compared in the problem differ only in ion concentrations, explaining the EMF difference
Summary
Electrode potential varies with ion concentration as described by the Nernst Equation. The thermodynamic basis is ΔG = −nFE; deviations from standard conditions shift Q, altering EMF. This accounts for the significant potential difference observed between the two cells in the problem.

Interpret the correct representation of an electrochemical cell using cell notation and infer how sign conventions determine electron flow direction and reaction feasibility.
6 Marks▼
Answer

Cell Notation Convention (IUPAC):

Anode (oxidation) | Anode solution || Cathode solution | Cathode (reduction)

Example for Daniell Cell:

Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)

Rules:

• A single vertical line (|) represents a phase boundary (electrode-electrolyte interface).
• A double vertical line (||) represents the salt bridge.
• The anode (oxidation) is always written on the left; cathode (reduction) on the right.
• Electrons flow from left (anode) to right (cathode) through the external circuit.

Sign Convention for EMF:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

• If E°cell > 0: reaction is spontaneous, electrons flow from anode to cathode.
• If E°cell < 0: reaction is non-spontaneous; cell cannot operate without external energy.
• Misinterpreting the sign leads to wrong prediction of electron flow direction.

Student Error: If the student incorrectly places the cathode on the left, E°cell will appear negative, wrongly suggesting non-spontaneity and reversed electron flow. The IUPAC convention must be strictly followed.

Key Points
  • Anode on left, cathode on right — IUPAC convention
  • Single line = phase boundary; Double line = salt bridge
  • E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
  • Positive EMF → spontaneous reaction → electrons flow left to right
  • Sign convention errors → wrong electron flow direction prediction
Summary
Cell notation uses IUPAC conventions: anode on left, cathode on right, with | and || symbols. EMF sign (positive = spontaneous) determines electron flow direction. Misinterpreting cell notation leads to incorrect conclusions about spontaneity and current direction.

Analyze why a reference electrode is necessary and determine how the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) enables measurement of electrode potential.
6 Marks▼
Answer

Why Absolute Potential Cannot Be Measured:
The absolute potential of a single electrode cannot be measured because all potential measurements require a complete circuit with two electrodes. We can only measure the potential difference between two half-cells.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) as Reference:
The SHE is universally accepted as the reference electrode and is assigned a potential of exactly 0.00 V. It consists of a platinum electrode in contact with hydrogen gas at 1 atm pressure and an aqueous solution with H⁺ activity = 1 M (pH = 0).

2H⁺(aq, 1M) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g, 1 atm) E° = 0.00 V

Measurement Procedure:
When the unknown electrode is connected to SHE and the cell EMF (E°cell) is measured:
• If the unknown electrode is the cathode: E°unknown = E°cell
• If the unknown electrode is the anode: E°unknown = −E°cell

Role of Platinum in SHE: Platinum is inert (does not participate in the reaction) but acts as a catalyst to facilitate the reversible oxidation/reduction of hydrogen, ensuring accurate equilibrium potential is established.

By measuring all electrode potentials against SHE, we can compare them on a universal scale — the electrochemical series.

Key Points
  • Absolute electrode potential cannot be measured; only relative potential possible
  • SHE is assigned E° = 0.00 V at standard conditions
  • SHE conditions: Pt electrode, [H⁺] = 1 M, P(H₂) = 1 atm, 298 K
  • E°unknown = E°cell (when SHE is anode) or −E°cell (when SHE is cathode)
  • Enables creation of the electrochemical series
Summary
The SHE provides a universal zero reference (E° = 0.00 V) against which all electrode potentials are measured. By coupling the unknown electrode with SHE and measuring the cell EMF, the standard electrode potential of any half-cell can be determined.

Interpret the origin of interfacial potential at a metal-electrolyte interface and infer the role of equilibrium in its formation.
6 Marks▼
Answer

Origin of Interfacial Potential:
When a metal electrode is immersed in an electrolyte containing its own ions, a dynamic equilibrium is established at the interface:

M(s) ⇌ Mⁿ⁺(aq) + ne⁻

Initially, metal atoms at the surface tend to oxidize and dissolve into solution as cations (tendency depends on the metal's ionization tendency). As metal ions enter the solution, the metal surface acquires a negative charge (excess electrons). This negative charge attracts cations from solution back to the surface, setting up an electrical double layer.

Helmholtz Double Layer: The interface consists of:
• A layer of negative charge on the metal surface
• A layer of positive ions in the solution adjacent to the metal

This charge separation creates an electric potential difference across the interface — called the electrode potential or Galvani potential.

Role of Equilibrium:
At equilibrium, the rate of metal dissolution equals the rate of ion deposition. No net current flows, yet a stable, measurable potential exists across the interface. This is the equilibrium electrode potential. It is a thermodynamic property determined by the activity of ions in solution and temperature.

Key Points
  • Interfacial potential arises from charge separation at the metal-electrolyte interface
  • Metal oxidizes → negative charge on metal; positive ions accumulate in solution
  • This forms the electrical double layer
  • At equilibrium: forward rate = backward rate; net current = 0, but potential ≠ 0
  • Potential is a thermodynamic quantity — related to ion activity by Nernst equation
Summary
Interfacial potential arises from the tendency of metal atoms to oxidize, creating a charge separation (double layer) at the interface. At equilibrium, dissolution and deposition rates are equal, no net current flows, yet a stable potential exists — determined by ion activity and temperature.

Determine how ionic movement supports sustained electron flow in a closed electrochemical circuit.
4 Marks▼
Answer

In an electrochemical cell, current flows through two pathways simultaneously:

1. Electronic conduction (external circuit):
Electrons produced at the anode (oxidation) flow through the metallic wire to the cathode (reduction). This constitutes the conventional external current.

2. Ionic conduction (through electrolyte):
As oxidation at the anode releases cations into solution (e.g., Zn → Zn²⁺), the anode compartment becomes positively charged. Simultaneously, at the cathode, cations (Cu²⁺) are deposited, making the cathode compartment negatively charged. To prevent charge buildup:

• Cations in the electrolyte migrate toward the cathode (toward negative charge)
• Anions migrate toward the anode (toward positive charge)

This ionic migration through the salt bridge or electrolyte maintains electrical neutrality and allows the circuit to be completed. Without ionic movement, charge imbalance would immediately stop electron flow. Thus, electron flow and ionic flow are coupled — neither can sustain without the other.

Key Points
  • Two types of current: electronic (external wire) and ionic (electrolyte)
  • Cations move toward cathode; anions move toward anode
  • Salt bridge ensures charge neutrality in both compartments
  • Without ionic movement → charge accumulation → reaction stops
Summary
Ionic movement through the electrolyte/salt bridge completes the electrical circuit. Cations migrate toward the cathode and anions toward the anode, maintaining charge neutrality and enabling sustained electron flow through the external circuit.

Infer why an electrolyte is essential for current generation in electrochemical systems when two different metals are connected without an electrolyte.
4 Marks▼
Answer

When two different metals are connected by a wire only (no electrolyte), no current flows despite a potential difference existing between them. This is because:

1. Incomplete circuit:
For current to flow in an electrochemical cell, the circuit must be complete — both electronically (through the wire) and ionically (through the electrolyte). Without an electrolyte, the ionic part of the circuit is absent.

2. No redox reaction:
Electrochemical reactions (oxidation at anode, reduction at cathode) require an ionic medium to accept or release ions. Without electrolyte, the metal cannot dissolve (no acceptor for metal ions) and no reduction can occur (no ions to be reduced).

3. No charge transfer:
The electrolyte provides ions that physically carry charge between the two electrodes, balancing the electron flow through the wire. Without ions, charge accumulates and reaction halts.

Conclusion: The electrolyte serves as the ionic conductor that completes the circuit, enables redox reactions, and allows sustained current generation.

Key Points
  • Electrolyte = ionic conductor; essential to complete the circuit
  • Without electrolyte: no ionic conduction → no complete circuit → no current
  • Electrolyte enables oxidation and reduction reactions at respective electrodes
  • Metal alone cannot sustain charge balance without ionic medium
Summary
An electrolyte is essential because it provides the ionic conduction pathway to complete the circuit. Without it, the redox reactions at the electrodes cannot proceed, charge balance cannot be maintained, and no sustained current can flow.

Analyze the difference between measured potentials under open-circuit and load conditions and determine the role of internal resistance and system equilibrium.
8 Marks▼
Answer

Open-Circuit Potential (OCP):
When no current is drawn, the cell operates at thermodynamic equilibrium. The measured voltage equals the true EMF of the cell. No overpotentials or IR drops are present.

V_OCP = EMF = E°cell (under standard conditions)

Terminal Voltage Under Load:
When current (I) is drawn through an external load, the voltage drops below the OCP due to:

1. Internal Resistance (r):
Every practical cell has internal resistance due to electrolyte resistance, electrode resistance, and contact resistance. When current flows, a voltage drop occurs across this resistance:

V_terminal = EMF − I × r

2. Polarization (Overpotential):
During current flow, the electrode reactions deviate from equilibrium. This deviation is called overpotential (η). Three types occur:
• Activation overpotential — energy barrier for charge transfer
• Concentration overpotential — ion concentration gradient near electrode
• Ohmic overpotential — resistance losses

The total terminal voltage:

V_terminal = EMF − η_anode − η_cathode − I × r

Role of Equilibrium:
At open circuit, both forward and reverse reactions occur equally — system is at equilibrium. Under load, the reactions are driven in one direction, breaking equilibrium and causing polarization losses.

Key Points
  • Open-circuit voltage = true EMF (equilibrium state)
  • Terminal voltage < EMF due to internal resistance and overpotential
  • V = EMF − Ir (simple form)
  • Higher current → larger IR drop → lower terminal voltage
  • Overpotential: activation + concentration + ohmic types
Summary
OCP represents equilibrium EMF. Under load, terminal voltage drops due to internal resistance (V = EMF − Ir) and polarization effects (overpotentials). The greater the current drawn, the larger the deviation from the ideal EMF.

Determine how variations in concentration, temperature, and pressure influence the observed EMF and justify the combined effect using electrochemical reasoning.
8 Marks▼
Answer

The EMF of a cell depends on temperature (T), concentration (C), and pressure (P) as described by the Nernst Equation:

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

1. Effect of Concentration:
For a reaction: aA + bB → cC + dD

Q = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ

• Increasing reactant concentration → Q decreases → E increases (higher EMF)
• Increasing product concentration → Q increases → E decreases (lower EMF)
• In the industrial setup, continuous consumption of reactants increases Q over time, reducing EMF.

2. Effect of Temperature:
Temperature affects EMF in two ways:
• Directly through the RT/nF term in Nernst equation — higher T increases the correction term
• Through the temperature coefficient of EMF:

ΔS = nF × (∂E/∂T)_P

For endothermic reactions (ΔH > 0), EMF increases with temperature. For exothermic reactions, EMF decreases with temperature.

3. Effect of Pressure (for gas electrodes):
For gas electrodes (e.g., H₂ electrode), pressure affects the activity of the gas:

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln [1/(P_H₂)] → E increases with increasing P_H₂

Combined: All three factors simultaneously influence the reaction quotient Q and hence the EMF. In the industrial setup described, all three fluctuate together, causing continuous variation in measured cell potential.

Key Points
  • Concentration: ↑ reactant → ↑ EMF; ↑ product → ↓ EMF (Nernst equation)
  • Temperature: effect depends on sign of ΔS; endothermic → EMF ↑ with T
  • Pressure: affects gas electrode potentials; ↑ P_H₂ → ↑ EMF for H₂ electrode
  • All three factors modify Q in the Nernst equation
  • Industrial cells show EMF fluctuations due to all three varying simultaneously
Summary
EMF depends on concentration (via Q in Nernst equation), temperature (via RT/nF term and ΔS), and pressure (affects gas electrode activities). All three simultaneously modify the reaction quotient, causing the observed EMF fluctuations in industrial electrochemical setups.

Analyze the differences between reversible and irreversible cells and infer how these differences affect the relationship between EMF and Gibbs free energy.
8 Marks▼
Answer

Reversible Electrochemical Cell:
A cell is thermodynamically reversible when it operates infinitely slowly, maintaining equilibrium at every step. Characteristics:
• Infinitesimally small current flows
• No energy loss as heat
• The reaction can be completely reversed by applying the same EMF in the opposite direction
• Maximum electrical work is obtained

Irreversible Cell:
In rapid discharge, the system deviates from equilibrium. Characteristics:
• Large current flows → significant IR drops and overpotentials
• Energy is dissipated as heat
• Reaction cannot be exactly reversed
• Actual electrical work output < maximum work

Relationship with Gibbs Free Energy:

For reversible cell: ΔG = −nFE_rev (maximum work obtainable)
For irreversible cell: W_actual < −ΔG

The Gibbs free energy represents the maximum useful work that a system can perform at constant T and P. Only a perfectly reversible cell can fully convert ΔG into electrical work.

−ΔG = nFE (reversible) → Efficiency = W_actual / −ΔG × 100%

For the two cells compared: the slowly operated reversible cell has higher EMF and efficiency; the rapidly discharged irreversible cell shows lower voltage due to polarization losses and energy dissipation as heat.

Key Points
  • Reversible cell: slow process, equilibrium maintained, max work = −ΔG = nFE
  • Irreversible cell: fast, energy lost as heat, actual work < −ΔG
  • ΔG = −nFE strictly holds only for reversible processes
  • Efficiency = actual work / −ΔG × 100%
  • All real cells are irreversible to some degree
Summary
Reversible cells operate at equilibrium with maximum efficiency: ΔG = −nFE. Irreversible cells involve energy losses (heat, overpotential), so actual work < −ΔG. The degree of irreversibility determines how far the cell's practical output deviates from the thermodynamic maximum.

Electrochemical Series:
The electrochemical series (activity series) is an arrangement of metals in order of their standard electrode potentials (E°) measured against SHE at standard conditions (25°C, 1M, 1 atm).

Measurement:
The unknown metal (M) is coupled with SHE. The measured E°cell gives E° of the metal:

If M acts as cathode (more noble): E°M = E°cell

Position in Series & Reactivity:

• More negative E°: Metal is placed higher in the series (more reactive, stronger reducing agent, greater tendency to oxidize/corrode)

Predictive Use:
• A metal higher in series displaces metals lower in series from their salt solutions
• Used to predict corrosion tendency, displacement reactions, and cell feasibility

Key Points
  • More negative E° → more reactive, placed higher in electrochemical series
  • SHE provides the universal reference (E° = 0.00 V)
  • Series predicts: displacement reactions, corrosion, cell spontaneity
  • Metal with lower E° (more negative) acts as anode in galvanic cell
Summary
EMF measured vs SHE gives the standard electrode potential of the unknown metal. More negative E° = more reactive, placed higher in electrochemical series. The series predicts reactivity, displacement reactions, and corrosion tendency.

Answer

Standard EMF (E°cell) is the EMF measured when all species are at unit activity (1 M concentration for solutions, 1 atm for gases, pure solids/liquids) at 298 K.

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

  • Standard conditions: 1M, 1 atm, 298 K, unit activity
  • E°cell > 0 → spontaneous; E°cell < 0 → non-spontaneous
  • Used to calculate ΔG° and equilibrium constant K
  • Provides universal basis for comparing cells
Summary
Standard EMF provides a universal reference for comparing electrochemical systems at unit activity conditions. It directly relates to ΔG° and K, enabling prediction of reaction spontaneity and equilibrium position without needing to run the actual experiment.

Identify the limitation of measuring EMF using a voltmeter directly on a working cell and infer a more appropriate method for accurate EMF determination.
4 Marks▼
Answer

Limitation of Voltmeter Method:
A voltmeter draws a small but finite current when connected to a cell. When current flows:

V_measured = EMF − I × r (terminal voltage, not true EMF)

Since r > 0 and I > 0, V_measured < EMF. The voltage drop across internal resistance gives an inaccurate (lower) reading. Additionally, when the cell is simultaneously supplying current to a load, the true equilibrium condition is disturbed, causing polarization and further reducing the measured value.

Accurate Method: Potentiometer Method
The Poggendorff potentiometer measures EMF by a null deflection method — at balance point, no current is drawn from the cell. Since I = 0:

V_measured = EMF − I×r = EMF − 0 = EMF

The potentiometer opposes the cell EMF with an equal and opposite known EMF until the galvanometer reads zero. At this null point, the measured voltage equals the true EMF without any IR drop. This gives an accurate, reproducible EMF value.

Key Points
  • Voltmeter draws current → V = EMF − Ir → underestimates EMF
  • Under load, additional polarization further reduces reading
  • Potentiometer (null method): no current drawn → true EMF measured
  • Null deflection condition: galvanometer reads zero
Summary
A voltmeter gives inaccurate EMF because it draws current, causing an IR drop. The potentiometer (null method) draws no current at balance, giving V_measured = EMF exactly, making it the preferred method for accurate EMF determination.

Analyze how different theoretical models interpret the structure of the electrical double layer and justify which model best reconciles compact and diffuse charge distributions.
8 Marks▼
Answer

The Electrical Double Layer (EDL) is the region at the electrode-electrolyte interface where charge separation occurs. Three key models describe its structure:

1. Helmholtz Model (1853):
The earliest model — treats the double layer as a simple parallel plate capacitor. The electrode has a charge σ and an equal but opposite layer of ions lines up exactly at a fixed distance (Helmholtz plane) in solution.

C = ε₀εᵣA/d (capacitance like a parallel plate capacitor)

Limitation: Ignores thermal motion; fails at high temperatures; cannot explain diffuse behavior of ions.

2. Gouy-Chapman Model (1910–1913):
Accounts for thermal motion of ions. Ions are distributed diffusely (Boltzmann distribution) rather than in a fixed layer. Concentration of ions decreases exponentially from the surface into the bulk.

Limitation: Overestimates capacitance at high potentials; treats ions as point charges (unrealistic at high concentrations).

3. Stern Model (1924) — Best Model:
Combines both models. The EDL has two regions:
• Compact (Stern) layer: fixed ions adsorbed close to electrode (like Helmholtz)
• Diffuse layer: thermally distributed ions extending into bulk (like Gouy-Chapman)

1/C_total = 1/C_Stern + 1/C_diffuse

The Stern model best reconciles both compact and diffuse behavior, especially at varying temperatures and concentrations. At high temperatures, the diffuse layer expands; at low temperatures/high concentrations, the Stern (compact) layer dominates.

Key Points
  • Helmholtz: rigid parallel layers; fails at high T
  • Gouy-Chapman: diffuse distribution; fails at high concentrations
  • Stern model: combines both — compact + diffuse layers; most accurate
  • EDL capacitance: 1/C = 1/C_Stern + 1/C_diffuse
  • Higher temperature → thicker diffuse layer
Summary
The Stern model is the most comprehensive, combining Helmholtz's compact layer with Gouy-Chapman's diffuse layer. It correctly predicts EDL behavior under varying temperature and concentration conditions, explaining why the rigid layer assumption fails at high temperatures.

Determine how the potential gradient develops across the electrical double layer and evaluate its relationship with charge distribution and capacitance at the interface.
6 Marks▼
Answer

Potential Gradient Across EDL:
The potential drops most steeply in the compact (Stern) layer near the electrode surface, where the electric field is strongest. In the diffuse layer, the potential drops exponentially toward the bulk solution value (zero or the reference potential).

φ(x) = φ₀ × e^(−κx) (in the diffuse layer, Debye-Hückel approximation)

Where κ = Debye-Hückel parameter (inverse Debye length); higher ionic concentration → larger κ → thinner diffuse layer → steeper potential drop.

Charge Distribution:
• High charge density in the compact layer (adsorbed ions)
• Exponentially decaying charge density in the diffuse layer
• Total surface charge = integral of charge in both layers (electroneutrality)

Double Layer Capacitance:
The EDL behaves as a capacitor. Capacitance C is related to charge Q and potential φ:

C = dQ/dφ (differential capacitance)

For Stern model, the total capacitance is a series combination. The non-linear potential profile near the electrode explains the non-linear capacitance vs. potential curve (electrocapillary curve — minimum at potential of zero charge, PZC).

Key Points
  • Steepest potential drop in compact Stern layer near electrode
  • Exponential decay of potential in diffuse layer
  • Debye length (1/κ) characterizes thickness of diffuse layer
  • High ionic strength → thin diffuse layer, high capacitance
  • C = dQ/dφ (differential capacitance)
Summary
The potential gradient is steepest in the compact Stern layer and decays exponentially in the diffuse layer. Higher electrolyte concentration compresses the diffuse layer (shorter Debye length), increases capacitance, and creates a steeper overall potential drop near the electrode.

Interpret the combined influence of solvent orientation and specific ion adsorption on the effective structure and behavior of the electrical double layer.
6 Marks▼
Answer

Solvent Orientation (Inner Helmholtz Layer — IHL):
Polar solvent molecules (e.g., water) have permanent dipole moments. Near a charged electrode surface, these dipoles orient preferentially:
• Near a negatively charged electrode: water molecules orient with their positive end (H) toward the electrode
• Near a positively charged electrode: oxygen end faces the electrode

This ordered solvent layer — the Inner Helmholtz Plane (IHP) — forms even before ions adsorb, creating an additional dielectric layer that modifies the effective capacitance and potential distribution.

Specific Ion Adsorption:
Certain ions (especially large, polarizable anions like Cl⁻, I⁻, SCN⁻) can specifically adsorb onto the electrode surface, penetrating the solvent layer and contacting the metal directly. This is called specific adsorption or contact adsorption.

Effects of specific adsorption:
• Alters effective surface charge of electrode
• Shifts the potential of zero charge (PZC)
• Can cause overcharge — the adsorbed anions may make a net negatively charged surface appear positive in the diffuse layer
• Modifies EDL capacitance and affects electrode kinetics

Combined Effect: Solvent dipoles + specifically adsorbed ions together define the Inner Helmholtz Plane (IHP), while hydrated (non-specifically adsorbed) ions define the Outer Helmholtz Plane (OHP). Beyond OHP is the diffuse layer.

Key Points
  • Water dipoles orient near charged electrode → forms IHP
  • Specific adsorption: direct contact of polarizable ions with electrode surface
  • Specifically adsorbed ions alter effective surface charge and shift PZC
  • IHP (solvent + adsorbed ions) → OHP (hydrated ions) → Diffuse layer
  • Can cause charge reversal in the diffuse layer
Summary
Solvent orientation forms the IHP and modifies dielectric properties near the electrode. Specific ion adsorption alters effective surface charge and shifts PZC. Together, they define the compact layer structure (IHP + OHP), fundamentally modifying EDL behavior beyond what simple models predict.

Analyze how changes in the electrical double layer influence electrode kinetics and reaction rates when electrolyte concentration is varied.
6 Marks▼
Answer

The electrical double layer (EDL) directly controls the rate of electrode reactions through three mechanisms:

1. Frumkin Effect (Double Layer Effect on Kinetics):
The reacting species must cross the EDL to reach the electrode surface. The potential within the diffuse layer (φ₂ potential at OHP) acts as an additional barrier or driving force for charged reactants. This modifies the effective activation energy for electron transfer.

i = i₀ × exp[αF(E − φ₂)/RT] (corrected Butler-Volmer equation)

• If concentration gradient and electric field drive ions in the same direction: fluxes add → enhanced transport
• If they act in opposite directions: net flux is reduced → transport limited

  • At equilibrium, NP equation reduces to Nernst equation
  • Used for modeling electrode processes, sensors, batteries
